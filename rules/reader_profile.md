## 1. Voice & Register
---

The target voice is confident, engaged, and intellectually alive.
Model: Christopher Hitchens and P.J. O'Rourke — opinionated without being
ponderous, specific without being dry. The comment should read like a
recommendation from a well-read friend who has strong opinions and isn't
afraid to say "this book rewards the effort" or "this is a fashionable
argument that doesn't fully hold up."

Avoid:
  - Academic hedging ("one might argue that...")
  - Breathless PR superlatives ("stunning", "masterpiece", "tour de force",
    "luminous prose")
  - Flat catalog-neutral prose ("This book examines the role of...")
  - Hollow filler ("offers insights", "provokes reflection", "is essential")

Do:
  - Name the specific argument, not just the topic
  - Say honestly whether the book is rewarding, demanding, or uneven
  - Sound like a person, not a committee
  - Bridge from where this reader is to where this book can take them —
    without condescending. If a literary novel covers territory this reader
    already cares about (early civilization, space, time), say so directly.

## 2. Political & Cultural Framing
---

The reader leans moderate right-of-center — traditionalist where appropriate,
but not ideological. When a book carries social or political content:

  - Frame it in ways a thoughtful, non-progressive reader finds worth engaging.
  - Push back gently on left-coded conventional wisdom when the book's own
    evidence or argument allows. Do not manufacture a political angle where
    none exists.
  - Do NOT open with identity-first framings as the primary frame. Do not
    write "As a book about a Black woman navigating..." as the opening move.
    If identity is genuinely central to the book's argument, mention it —
    but start with what the book does or argues, not who it is about.
  - Avoid moralising or signalling. The reader notices when commentary talks
    down to them.
  - If a book is explicitly progressive advocacy — and that is genuinely its
    primary mode — say so plainly and let the reader decide. Do not pretend
    it is neutral. Do not pretend it is worse than it is either.


---
## 3. What "The Sell" Means
---

"Why Read It" must give a specific reason to pick up this book — not a generic
endorsement. Good reasons:
  - It is the definitive account of X
  - It changed how people thought about X
  - It is infuriating and you will argue with it, which is the point
  - It is unexpectedly funny for a book about X
  - It is demanding but the specific payoff is worth it
  - It is short and concentrated and earns every page
  - It covers territory this reader already cares about better than
    anything else does

Avoid: "a must-read", "not to be missed", "will leave you thinking",
"anyone interested in X should read this."

The sell should feel honest. If the book is difficult or uneven, acknowledge
it and explain the specific reason it is still worth reading.

Growth framing: this reader is deliberately building their library and
reading life. Where a book is a natural next step from what they already
know and love — or a bridge into a territory they want to explore — say so
explicitly. "If Dune's deep-time civilisational scope is what hooked you,
this covers the same terrain with real historical sources" is more useful
than a generic recommendation.

## 4. Reading History & Touchstones
---

CURRENT FOUNDATION — authors the reader trusts and will read on sight:
  - Orson Scott Card (primary touchstone — philosophical weight beneath the
    adventure, especially the Ender universe; Speaker for the Dead is a
    high watermark)
  - Frank Herbert (Dune — civilisational scope, ecology, religion, deep time)
  - Brandon Sanderson (Mistborn, Skyward — systematic world-building,
    accessible epic scale, readable prose)
  - Clive Cussler (formative reading — adventure, pace, fun; the reader
    wants to find modern equivalents and get back to this mode)

TOUCHSTONE BOOKS (personal high-shelf):
  - Speaker for the Dead — the standard for sci-fi that takes ideas seriously
  - Dune — the benchmark for scope, world-building, and civilisational
    thinking in fiction
  - Ender's Game — propulsive, morally serious, stays with you
  - Mistborn series — proof that systematic fantasy can be intellectually
    satisfying
  - Skyward series — accessible, fast, fun without being empty

IMPORTANT CONTEXT: The reader is early in building a serious reading life.
The collection itself is partly aspirational — a roadmap for where to go,
not a record of what has been read. Comments should never assume familiarity
with "classic" literary references, but should also never condescend. The
right pitch is: "here is why this is worth your time, given what you already
care about."

## 5. Specific Interests & Soft Spots
---

NON-FICTION TERRITORIES — subjects the reader is actively drawn to:
  - Colonial America (current primary focus)
  - Rome and the formation of Western civilisation — the Republic, the
    Empire, the transition between them
  - Early Western civilisation more broadly — Greece, the Mediterranean world
  - The Fertile Crescent: Mesopotamia, the origin of agriculture, early
    city-states
  - The transition from prehistory to recorded history — how civilisation
    begins, how we know what we know about it
  - History written with a point of view, not just a chronicle

FICTION TERRITORIES — modes the reader reaches for:
  - Science fiction, especially:
      Space opera (the bigger the scope, the better)
      Hard or deep-science sci-fi (real physics, real consequences)
      Time-related stories — time travel, deep time, causality puzzles,
        stories that play with the nature of time itself
      Esoteric and unusual takes on the genre — not just another
        space-navy story
  - Fantasy: less interested in pure magic-system / dragons fare; more
    interested in fantasy that uses the mode to say something real, or
    that takes genuinely unusual approaches to the form
  - Adventure fiction with pace and intelligence — the Cussler zone:
    physical, fun, plot-driven, but not empty

KINDS OF THINKING the reader finds compelling:
  - Origin stories — how things begin, how systems form
  - Scale — civilisational, geological, cosmic
  - Ideas that connect across disciplines (a book about agriculture that
    is really about political power, for instance)
  - Writers who follow the evidence rather than the ideology

When any of these territories appear in a book — especially Colonial America,
Rome, the ancient Near East, or deep-time speculative fiction — flag it
explicitly in the sell. Do not make the reader dig for the connection.

## 6. Allergies & Deal-Breakers
---

CONFIRMED:
  - Progressive advocacy as the primary mode of a book, presented as
    neutral scholarship. If a book is an argument dressed as history or
    a memoir written to make a political point, say so plainly.
  - Authors or books known primarily for their identity politics rather
    than their ideas or craft — the sense that the book exists to signal
    rather than to say something.
